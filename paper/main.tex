\begin{abstract}
The maximum $k$-defective edge biclique ($k$-MDB) problem is an emerging NP-hard optimization. We propose novel graph reduction techniques and adapt Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) to the k-MDB problem. In many instances, ACO produces bicliques with $80\%$ or more edges than previously proposed heuristics. Additionally, ACO provides a tighter lower bound on the size of the $k$-MDB and can cut brute force search time by more than half. Finally, we identify novel graph reduction techniques that significantly reduce graph size compared to previous methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Bipartite graphs can model user-product behavior, person-place interaction, and more. The maximum edge biclique problem aims to identify the biclique with the most edges on a bipartite graph. The problem has applications in online recommendation systems, fraud detection, and community detection~\cite{cui}. However, real-world data often introduces noise to graphs, and structures with relatively few missing connections may still be relevant. A biclique that is allowed up to $k$ missing edges, or a $k$-defective biclique ($k$-DB), can overcome this real-world noise.

Similar dense subgraph models provide precedent for the $k$-MDB. In unipartite graphs, \emph{defective cliques}, cliques that are missing up to a certain number of edges, have been used to recover nearly complete structures from noisy biological interaction graphs~\cite{yu2006}. On bipartite graphs, relaxed models include $k$-biplexes, which permit each vertex up to $k$ nonneighbors in the biplex~\cite{yu2023}, and quasi-bicliques, which allows a maximum of $\gamma\%$ edges to be missing~\cite{TODO_quasi_biclique}. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025} studied the problem of finding a $k$-defective biclique with the largest vertex count rather than edge count, which is also an NP-hard problem. The problem we study is similar to theirs, but it maximizes the number of edges. The $k$-defective maximum edge biclique generalizes to the maximum edge biclique when $k = 0$~\cite{cui,wang2025}.

Maximizing edge count alone is not enough for real-world interpretability. Without a lower bound on each side's size, the optimum is often a lopsided subgraph with many edges but little practical significance. In the case of fraud, a single popular item purchased by thousands of unrelated users could be a massive biclique with one vertex opposite many others, but it signals mass-market demand rather than a coordinated ring. We therefore seek only $\theta$-feasible \mbox{$k$-DB}: \mbox{$k$-DB} where both sides have at least $\theta$ vertices and $\theta$ is a user-defined parameter.

Dorigo et al.~\cite{dorigo1996} proposed a metaheuristic known as Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) for exploring graphs. ACO is a natural metaheuristic for problems like maximum clique~\cite{solnon2006} or $k$-MDB where solutions are built by adding each vertex one at a time. We introduce an ACO heuristic for the $k$-defective maximum edge biclique ($k$-MDB) problem. ACO frequently finds larger and more often $\theta$-feasible solutions than the existing $\theta$-heuristic, although at substantially higher computational cost. We further show that these stronger solutions can improve branch-and-pivot search. Together, these results demonstrate ACO as a complementary approach to existing $k$-MDB heuristics, trading increased search cost for improved solution quality.

The $k$-MDB is an emerging NP-hard problem. To our knowledge, the $\theta$-heuristic of Cui et al.~\cite{cui} is the only published heuristic algorithm for the $k$-MDB problem. ACO is therefore compared against the only existing heuristic baseline in the literature.

Exact algorithms for dense subgraph problems rarely run on the raw input graph. They first apply reduction rules that delete vertices or edges that provably cannot appear in a valid solution, and then search the smaller graph that remains. Because the cost of branching grows exponentially with the number of vertices, even modest reductions can substantially speed up search. Reductions have been used for maximum clique \cite{TODO_clique_reduction}, maximum (edge) biclique \cite{TODO_biclique_reduction}, and relaxed variants such as $k$-plexes and $k$-biplexes \cite{TODO_kplex_reduction}.

For the $k$-MDB problem, Cui et al.~\cite{cui} propose vertex and edge pruning methods. We propose novel redution methods that build on their work. By mapping a single side of the bipartite graph onto a graph, where edges denote nodes that could potentially be in a $k$-DB, we can significantly reduce graph size. This leads to speed improvements, and because it reduces the number of potential subgraphs, it improves quality as well.

\section{Definitions}

Define a bipartite graph as a graph $G = (U, V, E)$, where $U$ and $V$ are two disjoint sets of vertices, and $E \subseteq U \times V$ is the set of edges between $U$ and $V$. Let $S = (U_S, V_S)$ denote a set of vertices where $U_S \subseteq U$ and $V_S \subseteq V$. Let $E_S = \{(u, v) \mid u \in U_S \wedge v \in V_S \wedge (u, v) \in E \}$ be the set of edges in a subgraph $S$. Define $\bar{E}(S) = \{(u, v) \mid u \in U_S \wedge v \in V_S \wedge (u, v) \not\in E \}$ be the set of non-edges in a subgraph. Let a $k$-defective biclique be a subgraph $S$ where $|\bar{E}(S)| \leq k$.

Define the neighbors of a vertex $u$ in a subgraph $S$ as $N_S(u) = \{v \in S \mid \{u, v\} \in E(S)\}$. Define the nonneighbors of a vertex as $\bar{N}_S(u) = \{v \in S \mid (u, v) \not\in E(S)\}$. Define the degree of a vertex as $d_S(u) = |N_S(u)|$, and the nondegree as $\bar{d}_S(u) = |\bar{N}_S(u)|$.

Define the maximum $k$-MDB as the $k$-DB with the largest number of edges in a graph $G$. Let $\theta$ be a user-defined minimum side size. The algorithms proposed in this paper seek the maximum $k$-MDB $D^*$ with $|U_{D^*}| \geq \theta$ and $|V_{D^*}| \geq \theta$ as described in Section~1. We require that $\theta > k$ so that vertices must be connected to at least one vertex in the subgraph to be included in a $k$-MDB. The maximum clique reduces to the $k$-MDB, making the optimization variant of the latter NP-hard~\cite{cui}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Branch-and-Pivot Approach}

As a baseline against ACO, we implement the approach proposed by Cui et al.~\cite{cui}. These authors observe that many $\theta$-feasible $k$-DB have one side with exactly $\theta$ vertices. They therefore propose a heuristic that expands one side of the graph to $\theta$ vertices while removing vertices on the other side to respect the $k$ constraint. The $\theta$-heuristic has a time complexity of $\mathcal{O}(\theta n + m)$~\cite{cui}.

Cui et al.~\cite{cui} also propose a branch-and-pivot algorithm guaranteed to find the optimal solution The algorithm is able to prune more branches and run faster when given a tighter lower bound on the number of edges in the $k$-MDB, which they use the $\theta$-heuristic for.

\subsection{Graph Reduction Techniques}

Cui et al.~\cite{cui} propose common-neighbor reduction (CNR), which discards same-side vertex pairs with fewer than $\theta - k$ common neighbors, along with any vertex left with fewer than $\theta - k$ incident edges. We observe that CNR shrinks real-world graphs significantly, and use it as the base for the two reductions below. Both are slower than CNR, but they prune far more of the graph. A smaller graph also gives branch-and-pivot fewer vertices to branch on.

\subsubsection{One-Sided Representation}

A maximal $k$-DB is determined by the vertices on one of its sides. Fix $S_U \subseteq U$ with $|S_U| > k$. For $v \in V$, let its cost be the number of non-edges between $v$ and $S_U$:
\[
c(v \mid S_U) = |S_U| - |N(v) \cap S_U|.
\]
Because the non-edges of $v \in V$ are disjoint, the cost of a subset $S_V \subseteq V$ is $\sum_{v \in S_V}c(v \mid S_U)$. The best choice of $S_V$ is then to sort $V$ by cost and add vertices in ascending order until the total cost exceeds $k$. Both the reductions and ACO can therefore search over subsets of one side only. The edge count of the full biclique follows from the greedy fill.

\subsubsection{$\theta$ Neighbor Reduction}

Let $U$ be the smaller side, and let $C(u, u') = N(u) \cap N(u')$ be the common neighbors of two same-side vertices. Define the mapped graph $H = (U, \tilde{E})$, where $uu' \in \tilde{E}$ if $|C(u, u')| \ge \theta - k$.

If $u$ and $u'$ both appear in a $\theta$-feasible $k$-DB $D$, then each of the at least $\theta$ vertices in $V_D$ is adjacent to both, except for those incident to a missing edge. At most $k$ vertices can be, so $|C(u, u')| \ge \theta - k$. Therefore $U_D$ is a clique of at least $\theta$ vertices in $H$, which gives two pruning rules:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A vertex with degree less than $\theta - 1$ in $H$ cannot be in a clique of size $\theta$, and can be removed.
    \item An edge $uu'$ with fewer than $\theta - 2$ common neighbors in $H$ cannot be in a clique of size $\theta$, and can be removed.
\end{itemize}
Each removal can lower the degrees of other vertices, so both rules are applied until no vertex or edge changes.

\subsubsection{Missing Edge Reduction}

On the remaining edges of $H$, assign a weight
\[
w(u, u') = \max\bigl(0,\ \theta - |C(u, u')|\bigr).
\]
$|C(u, u')|$ is the largest number of edges that $u$ and $u'$ could contribute to a $k$-DB. $|V_D| \geq \theta$, so a lower bound on the number of missing edges that $u$ and $u'$ contribute to $D$ is $\theta - |C(u, u')|$. Because this is a lower bound, we floor this estimate at 0.

Take three vertices $u, u', z \in U_D$. $w(u, u')$ is the number of missing edges that $u$ and $u'$ would contribute, but $w(u, u') + w(u, z) + w(u', z)$ would overcount. For instance, if on a bipartite graph with three nodes each there is only one missing edge, the missing edge would be counted twice: once for $uu'$ and once for $uz$. For a given $u$, its missing edges may be overcounted on any other edge it's connected to. Because in the graph $\{u, u', z\}$, each node has exactly two edges, each edge may be overcounted by a maximum factor of $1/2$. In general, for a clique of size $N$, each node's missing edges might be overcounted by a factor of $N - 1$, and therefore the maximum missing edge count of a subgraph is $(N - 1)^{-1}\sum_{u, u' \in S}w(u, u')$ Hence
\[
|\bar{E}(D)| \ge \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(w(u, u') + w(u, z) + w(u', z)\bigr).
\]
Since $|U_D| \ge \theta \ge 3$, every $k$-DB containing $u$ and $u'$ contains some $z \in C_H(u, u')$, where $C_H$ is the common neighborhood in $H$. We therefore prune the edge $uu'$ if
\[
\min_{z \in C_H(u, u')} \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(w(u, u') + w(u, z) + w(u', z)\bigr) > k.
\]
As with $\theta$ neighbor reduction, this is repeated until the graph stops changing, since removing edges changes common neighborhoods and weights.

Additionally, if 
\[
|C_H(u, u') + 1|^{-1}\sum_{A, B \in C_H(u, u') \cup \{u,u'\}}w(A, B) > k
\]

the edge can be pruned by the same logic.

\subsection{Double-Greedy Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Double-Greedy Algorithm for $k$-MDB}
\label{alg:double-greedy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $G = (U, V, E)$; $k$, $\theta$
    \Ensure $S = (U_S, V_S)$, a $k$-DB of $G$

    \State $u \gets \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x \in U \cup V} d_G(x)$
    \State $S \gets (\{u\}, N(u))$ \textbf{if} $u \in U$ \textbf{else} $(N(u), \{u\})$

    \While{true}
        \If{$\min(|U_S|, |V_S|) < \theta$}
            \State $P \gets U \setminus U_S$ \textbf{if} $|U_S| \le |V_S|$ \textbf{else} $V \setminus V_S$
            \Comment{only grow the smaller side}
        \Else
            \State $P \gets (U \cup V) \setminus S$
        \EndIf

        \State $C \gets \{v \in P : |\bar{E}(S \cup \{v\})| \le k\}$
        \Comment{$k$-feasible candidates}
        \If{$C = \emptyset$}
            \State \textbf{break}
        \EndIf

        \State $v^* \gets \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{v \in C} |E(S \cup \{v\})|$
        \State $S \gets S \cup \{v^*\}$
        \Comment{$v^*$ joins $U_S$ if $v^* \in U$, else $V_S$}
    \EndWhile

    \State \Return $S$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Time Complexity}
The Double-Greedy algorithm scores every candidate node, which costs $\mathcal{O}(m)$. It repeats this process until it builds a large solution. Every step loops through the graph to generate a list of candidates,  and therefore a naive implementation has a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(n \times m)$. A memoized implementation can update the subgraph count by tracking $d_S$ for every vertex. Each time a vertex is added to the solution, its neighbors add $1$ to their $d_S$. The number of edges visited is equal to the total degree of each of the nodes in $S$, which is $\mathcal{O}(m)$. Each step loops over every $v \in G$ to generate a candidate step. The number of steps is equal to the size of the solution, which is $n$ in the worst case. Therefore, a memoized implementation has a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(n^2 + m)$. Finally, the maximum size of any one side of a $k$-DB is $\min(n, \Delta + k)$, where $\Delta$ is the maximum degree of a node. Therefore, naive implementation has a time complexity of $\mathcal{O}(\min(n, \Delta + k) \times m)$. Therefore, an optimized complexity is $\mathcal{O}(\min(n, \Delta + k) \times n + m)$, with space $\mathcal{O}(n)$.

\subsection{Clique-Edge Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Clique-Edge}
\label{alg:clique-edge}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

    \Require $G$
    \Ensure a $k$-DB of $G$

    \State $M \gets (V, E)$, a graph of every node in the smaller side of $G$, where edges exist between nodes if they share $\geq \theta - k$ common neighbors. Edge weights are equal to the number of common neighbors that two nodes share.

    \State $e \gets \max_{e \in E}w(e)$
    \State $S \gets $ the two endpoints of $e$
    \State $N \gets$ the endpoint of $e$ with the highest degree

    \While{true}
        \If{$N$ has at least one edge that has not been visited yet}
            \State $e \gets$ the edge connected to $N$ with the largest weight that has not been visited yet
        \Else
            \State $e \gets$ the edge connected to any node in $S$ with the largest weight that has not been visited yet
        \EndIf

        \If{$e$ could not be set}
            \State \textbf{break}
        \EndIf
    
        \State $P \gets$ the endpoint of $e$
        \If{$\bar{E}(S \cup \{P\}) \leq k$}
            \State add $P$ to $S$
            \State $N \gets P$
        \Else
            \State remove $P$ from $M$
        \EndIf
    \EndWhile

    \State \Return the greedy fill of $S$

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{ACO Algorithm}

\subsubsection{Overview of Algorithm}

ACO traditionally uses a set of agents, called ``ants'', that traverse a graph and leave pheromone on the vertices they visit, attracting other ants to follow their path. We adapt ACO by introducing a novel desirability function, and test ACO on a novel mapping of the bipartite graph.

A population of ants iteratively adds vertices from $G$ to a subgraph $S$, preferring vertices with higher subgraph degree $d_S$, higher global degree $d_G$, and higher pheromone $\tau$. An ant prefers to examine only the neighbors of the last vertex it visited. However, if every neighbor would violate the $k$ constraint, it defaults to sampling every $k$-feasible candidate.

The ACO algorithm is not guaranteed to find a $\theta$-feasible solution, even if one exists. This is a limitation of the $\theta$-heuristic as well (Section~3.2). However, like the $\theta$-heuristic, any returned solution is guaranteed $k$-feasible, and we empirically observe that ACO is more likely to return $\theta$-feasibly solutions.

\subsubsection{Algorithm Pseudocode}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:aco} is the colony loop. Here, $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+e^{-x})$ denotes the standard logistic sigmoid, used to dampen the impact of a vertex's degree with the entire graph as the size of the subgraph grows. We introduce a desirability function that prefers vertices with higher $d_S$, higher $d_G$, and higher pheromone $\tau$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Bipartite ACO for $k$-MDB}
\label{alg:aco}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $G$; $A$, $T$, $\rho$, $\Delta\tau$
    \Ensure $B$, a $k$-DB of $G$
    \Function{$\eta$}{$u, S_i$}
        \State \Return $\tau(u) \times (d_S(u) + d_G(u) \times (1 - \sigma(|U_S| + |V_S|)))^3$
    \EndFunction
    \State $\tau \gets \mathbf{1}$; $B \gets \emptyset$
    \For{$e = 1,\dots,T$}
        \For{$i = 1,\dots,A$ \textbf{in parallel}}
        \State $S_i \gets \emptyset$; $C \gets V(G)$
        \While{$C \neq \emptyset$}
            \State $P \gets C \cap N(v_{\mathrm{last}})$ \textbf{if} nonempty \textbf{else} $C$
            \State sample $u \in P$ with $\Pr[u] \propto \eta(u, S_i)$
            \State $S_i \gets S_i \cup \{u\}$
            \State $C \gets \{w \in C \setminus \{u\} : |\bar E(S_i \cup \{w\})| \le k\}$
        \EndWhile
        \EndFor
        \State $S^* \gets \arg\max_{S \in \{S_1,\dots,S_A\}} |E(S)|$
        \State $B \gets \arg\max_{S \in \{B, S^*\}} |E(S)|$
        \State $\tau(u) \gets \tau(u) + \Delta\tau$ \textbf{for all} $u \in S^*$
        \State $\tau \gets (1-\rho)\,\tau$
    \EndFor
    \State \Return $B$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Time Complexity}

Let $n = |U| + |V|$ and $m = |E|$ for a bipartite graph. Each ant must first examine $n$ vertices. A single node's neighbors may not have any $k$-feasible additions, so in the worst case, an ant must consider all vertices it can add to its subgraph at every step. It therefore examines at most $n + (n - 1) + (n - 2) \ldots = n(n + 1)/2$ nodes. Ants also run independently, only syncing pheromone at the end of each epoch, and the upper time complexity is therefore $\mathcal{O}(\lceil A / p \rceil \times T \times n^2)$ on a computer with $p$ processors. \subsection{Experimental Setup}

Unless otherwise noted, all ACO experiments use the following hyperparameter values: $A = 100, \rho = 0.05, \Delta\tau = 0.05, T = 5, R = 5$.

We use ``replicate'' to refer to an independent execution of the full ACO algorithm, with its own ant population and pheromone state. ACO is inherently stochastic, so independent replicates can explore different solutions. For each trial, a warmup replicate was discarded to avoid benchmarking the Julia JIT compilation. For comparisons against the deterministic $\theta$-heuristic, the reported ACO solution is the best solution obtained across these five independent replicates. Runtime comparisons include the cost of all five replicates. Section~4.1 examines how the number of replicates impacts runtime and solution quality.

We test on 37 graphs from two public collections: 28 from \href{http://konect.cc}{KONECT}, a standard repository of real-world bipartite networks; and 9 from \href{http://amazon-reviews-2023.github.io}{Amazon Reviews 2023}, user-product review graphs in selected product categories. All tests were run on a computer with Ubuntu, an Intel(R) Core(TM) Ultra 7 258V CPU, 8 threads, and 32 gigabytes of RAM.

\subsection{Branch-and-pivot seeding}

Runs where both graphs failed under a $600$s timeout were excluded.

We report all graphs where ACO seeding reduced total wall-clock time, plus the smallest and largest overheads among graphs where ACO did not outperform the $\theta$-heuristic. Time saved is the reduction in combined ACO seeding and branch-and-pivot runtime relative to $\theta$-heuristic seeding. Positive values indicate that ACO saved time.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}

While ACO is substantially slower than the $\theta$-heuristic, it often finds solutions several times larger in edge count. Even small improvements ACO makes over the $\theta$-heuristic can meaningfully reduce the time that branch-and-pivot takes. Additionally, ACO had a higher $\theta$-feasibility rate than the $\theta$-heuristic, meaning it can be used as a valid lower bound more often. In cases where runtime is more critical, ant count $A$, epoch budget $T$, and replicate count $R$ can be reduced for a linear effect on wall-clock time, at the expense of solution size and $\theta$-feasibility (Section~4.1). 

ACO improves quality in several types of solutions. On several graphs, both ACO and the $\theta$-heuristic find a solution with exactly $\theta$ nodes on one side, but ACO finds a substantially larger opposite side. In other cases, ACO identified $\theta \times \theta$ $k$-DB on graphs that the $\theta$-heuristic failed to make $\theta$-feasible. ACO may escape the $\theta$-exact limitation entirely, finding $k$-DB with both sides larger than $\theta$ nodes that the $\theta$-heuristic could not have found.

We hypothesize this is due to ACO's exploration. Because the $\theta$-heuristic arbitrarily chooses $U$ as its $\theta$-side and greedily expands, early vertex choices can permanently shrink the retained neighborhood. Conversely, ACO's stochastic, massively parallel construction samples alternative $\theta \times \theta$ cores and often recovers a better one. Thus, ACO often beats the $\theta$-heuristic even on structures the latter was designed for.

\subsection{Limitations}

As described in Section~3.3, because branch-and-pivot runtime scales exponentially with vertex count, our seeding experiment cannot yet confirm whether the pruning benefit observed on small graphs extends to larger instances. Additionally, we could not validate ACO's solution quality against the absolute optimum on large graphs. Finally, we injected bicliques with $\theta$ vertices on both sides into the graphs. The $\theta$-heuristic may have performed better with a more realistic biclique of $\gg \theta$ vertices on one side. However, our observations that ACO can beat the $\theta$-heuristic on subgraphs with $\gg \theta$ vertices on only one side demonstrate that ACO is inherently a more explorative heuristic.

\section{Conclusion}

Ant Colony Optimization is a promising heuristic approach to identifying large $k$-DB. As an emerging field, there do not exist many tested heuristics for this problem. The $\theta$-heuristic, the only previously proposed heuristic in the literature, is significantly faster than our algorithm but often does not produce solutions as large. ACO offers an easily tunable quality--runtime tradeoff. One instance in which extra quality is worth extra time is in branch-and-pivot, where ACO can function as a much more effective lower bound, cutting total wall-clock-time by up to $56\%$ on the tested instances.

\subsection{Future Work}

A natural next step would be to rerun the seeded branch-and-pivot comparison of Section~4.2 on higher-performance hardware, so that the larger graphs excluded under the timeout in Section~3.3 can be included and the scaling of the seeding benefit can be tested directly. Additionally, ACO could be adapted to have N subspecies that find N solutions in parallel by using a repulsion mechanism similar to that proposed for diversity in other ant colony variants~\cite{tfaili2008}, extending previous multi-colony parallelization strategies~\cite{middendorf2002}.
