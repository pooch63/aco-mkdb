\begin{abstract}
The maximum $k$-defective edge biclique ($k$-MDB) problem is an emerging NP-hard optimization. We propose novel graph reduction techniques and adapt Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) to the k-MDB problem. In many instances, ACO produces bicliques with $80\%$ or more edges than previously proposed heuristics. Additionally, ACO provides a tighter lower bound on the size of the $k$-MDB and can cut brute force search time by more than half. Finally, we identify novel graph reduction techniques that significantly reduce graph size compared to previous methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Bipartite graphs can model user-product behavior, person-place interaction, and more. The maximum edge biclique problem aims to identify the biclique with the most edges on a bipartite graph. The problem has applications in online recommendation systems, fraud detection, and community detection [1]. However, real-world data often introduces noise to graphs, and structures with relatively few missing connections may still be relevant. A biclique that is allowed up to $k$ missing edges, or a $k$-defective biclique ($k$-DB), can overcome this real-world noise.

Similar dense subgraph models provide precedent for the $k$-MDB. In unipartite graphs, \emph{defective cliques}, cliques that are missing up to a certain number of edges, have been used to recover nearly complete structures from noisy biological interaction graphs [12]. On bipartite graphs, relaxed models include $k$-biplexes, which permit each vertex up to $k$ nonneighbors in the biplex [15], and quasi-bicliques, which allows a maximum of $\gamma\%$ edges to be missing [citation needed]. Wang et al. [11] studied the problem of finding a $k$-defective biclique with the largest vertex count rather than edge count, which is also an NP-hard problem. The problem we study is similar to theirs, but it maximizes the number of edges. The $k$-defective maximum edge biclique generalizes to the maximum edge biclique when $k = 0$ [1], [11].

Maximizing edge count alone is not enough for real-world interpretability. Without a lower bound on each side's size, the optimum is often a lopsided subgraph with many edges but little practical significance. In the case of fraud, a single popular item purchased by thousands of unrelated users could be a massive biclique with one vertex opposite many others, but it signals mass-market demand rather than a coordinated ring. We therefore seek only $\theta$-feasible \mbox{$k$-DB}: \mbox{$k$-DB} where both sides have at least $\theta$ vertices, where $\theta$ is a user-defined parameter.

Dorigo et al. [2] proposed a metaheuristic known as Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) for exploring graphs. We introduce an ACO heuristic for the $k$-defective maximum edge biclique ($k$-MDB) problem. ACO frequently finds larger and more often $\theta$-feasible solutions than the existing $\theta$-heuristic, although at substantially higher computational cost. We further show that these stronger solutions can improve branch-and-pivot search. Together, these results demonstrate ACO as a complementary approach to existing $k$-MDB heuristics, trading increased search cost for improved solution quality.

Ant Colony Optimization is a natural metaheuristic for problems where high-quality solutions are built by iteratively adding vertices to a subgraph. In the maximum clique problem, ACO algorithms construct cliques greedily while using pheromone to reinforce vertices that are likely to appear in large solutions [8]. The $k$-MDB problem structure lends itself well to this algorithm: an ant can grow a near-biclique one vertex at a time, checking at each step whether the defect budget permits another addition.

The $k$-MDB is an emerging NP-hard problem. To our knowledge, the $\theta$-heuristic of Cui et al. [1] is the only published heuristic algorithm for the $k$-MDB problem. ACO is therefore compared against the only existing heuristic baseline in the literature.

\section{Definitions}

Define a bipartite graph as a graph $G = (U, V, E)$, where $U$ and $V$ are two disjoint sets of vertices, and $E \subseteq U \times V$ is the set of edges between $U$ and $V$. Let $S = (U_S, V_S)$ denote a set of vertices where $U_S \subseteq U$ and $V_S \subseteq V$. Let $E_S = \{(u, v) \mid u \in U_S \wedge v \in V_S \wedge (u, v) \in E \}$ be the set of edges in a subgraph $S$. Define $\bar{E}(S) = \{(u, v) \mid u \in U_S \wedge v \in V_S \wedge (u, v) \not\in E \}$ be the set of non-edges in a subgraph. Let a $k$-defective biclique be a subgraph $S$ where $|\bar{E}(S)| \leq k$.

Define the neighbors of a vertex $u$ in a subgraph $S$ as $N_S(u) = \{v \in S \mid \{u, v\} \in E(S)\}$. Define the nonneighbors of a vertex as $\bar{N}_S(u) = \{v \in S \mid (u, v) \not\in E(S)\}$. Define the degree of a vertex as $d_S(u) = |N_S(u)|$, and the nondegree as $\bar{d}_S(u) = |\bar{N}_S(u)|$.

Define the maximum $k$-MDB as the $k$-DB with the largest number of edges in a graph $G$. Let $\theta$ be a user-defined minimum side size. The algorithms proposed in this paper seek the maximum $k$-MDB $D^*$ with $|U_{D^*}| \geq \theta$ and $|V_{D^*}| \geq \theta$ as described in Section~1. We require that $\theta > k$ so that vertices must be connected to at least one vertex in the subgraph to be included in a $k$-MDB. The maximum clique reduces to the $k$-MDB, making the optimization variant of the latter NP-hard [1].

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Branch-and-Pivot Approach}

As a baseline against ACO, we implement the approach proposed by Cui et al. (2025). These authors observe that many $\theta$-feasible $k$-DB have one side with exactly $\theta$ vertices. They therefore propose a heuristic that expands one side of the graph to $\theta$ vertices while removing vertices on the other side to respect the $k$ constraint. The $\theta$-heuristic has a time complexity of $\mathcal{O}(\theta n + m)$ [1].

Cui et al. also propose a branch-and-pivot algorithm guaranteed to find the optimal solution The algorithm is able to prune more branches and run faster when given a tighter lower bound on the number of edges in the $k$-MDB, which they use the $\theta$-heuristic for.

\subsection{Graph Reduction Techniques}

Cui et al.\ propose common-neighbor reduction (CNR), which discards same-side vertex pairs with fewer than $\theta - k$ common neighbors, along with any vertex left with fewer than $\theta - k$ incident edges. We observe that CNR shrinks real-world graphs significantly, and use it as the base for the two reductions below. Both are slower than CNR, but they prune far more of the graph. A smaller graph also gives branch-and-pivot fewer vertices to branch on.

\subsubsection{One-Sided Representation}

A maximal $k$-DB is determined by the vertices on one of its sides. Fix $S_U \subseteq U$ with $|S_U| > k$. For $v \in V$, let its cost be the number of non-edges between $v$ and $S_U$:
\[
c(v \mid S_U) = |S_U| - |N(v) \cap S_U|.
\]
Because the non-edges of $v \in V$ are disjoint, the cost of a subset $S_V \subseteq V$ is $\sum_{v \in S_V}c(v \mid S_U)$. The best choice of $S_V$ is then to sort $V$ by cost and add vertices in ascending order until the total cost exceeds $k$. Both the reductions and ACO can therefore search over subsets of one side only. The edge count of the full biclique follows from the greedy fill.

\subsubsection{$\theta$ Neighbor Reduction}

Let $U$ be the smaller side, and let $C(u, u') = N(u) \cap N(u')$ be the common neighbors of two same-side vertices. Define the mapped graph $H = (U, \tilde{E})$, where $uu' \in \tilde{E}$ if $|C(u, u')| \ge \theta - k$.

If $u$ and $u'$ both appear in a $\theta$-feasible $k$-DB $D$, then each of the at least $\theta$ vertices in $V_D$ is adjacent to both, except for those incident to a missing edge. At most $k$ vertices can be, so $|C(u, u')| \ge \theta - k$. Therefore $U_D$ is a clique of at least $\theta$ vertices in $H$, which gives two pruning rules:
\begin{itemize}
    \item A vertex with degree less than $\theta - 1$ in $H$ cannot be in a clique of size $\theta$, and can be removed.
    \item An edge $uu'$ with fewer than $\theta - 2$ common neighbors in $H$ cannot be in a clique of size $\theta$, and can be removed.
\end{itemize}
Each removal can lower the degrees of other vertices, so both rules are applied until no vertex or edge changes.

\subsubsection{Missing Edge Reduction}

On the remaining edges of $H$, assign a weight
\[
w(u, u') = \max\bigl(0,\ \theta - |C(u, u')|\bigr).
\]
$|C(u, u')|$ is the largest number of edges that $u$ and $u'$ could contribute to a $k$-DB. $|V_D| \geq \theta$, so a lower bound on the number of missing edges that $u$ and $u'$ contribute to $D$ is $\theta - |C(u, u')|$. Because this is a lower bound, we floor this estimate at 0.

Take three vertices $u, u', z \in U_D$. $w(u, u')$ is the number of missing edges that $u$ and $u'$ would contribute, but $w(u, u') + w(u, z) + w(u', z)$ would overcount. For instance, if on a bipartite graph with three nodes each there is only one missing edge, the missing edge would be counted twice: once for $uu'$ and once for $uz$. For a given $u$, its missing edges may be overcounted on any other edge it's connected to. Because in the graph $\{u, u', z\}$, each node has exactly two edges, each edge may be overcounted by a maximum factor of $1/2$. In general, for a clique of size $N$, each node's missing edges might be overcounted by a factor of $N - 1$, and therefore the maximum missing edge count of a subgraph is $(N - 1)^{-1}\sum_{u, u' \in S}w(u, u')$ Hence
\[
|\bar{E}(D)| \ge \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(w(u, u') + w(u, z) + w(u', z)\bigr).
\]
Since $|U_D| \ge \theta \ge 3$, every $k$-DB containing $u$ and $u'$ contains some $z \in C_H(u, u')$, where $C_H$ is the common neighborhood in $H$. We therefore prune the edge $uu'$ if
\[
\min_{z \in C_H(u, u')} \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(w(u, u') + w(u, z) + w(u', z)\bigr) > k.
\]
As with $\theta$ neighbor reduction, this is repeated until the graph stops changing, since removing edges changes common neighborhoods and weights.

Additionally, take $C(u, u')$. If 
\[
|C(u, u')|^{-1}\sum_{A, B \in C(u, u')}w(A, B) > k
\]

the edge can be pruned by the same logic.

\subsection{ACO Algorithm}

\subsubsection{Overview of Algorithm}

ACO traditionally uses a set of agents, called ``ants'', that traverse a graph and leave pheromone on the vertices they visit, attracting other ants to follow their path. We adapt ACO by introducing a novel desirability function, and test ACO on a novel mapping of the bipartite graph.

A population of ants iteratively adds vertices from $G$ to a subgraph $S$, preferring vertices with higher subgraph degree $d_S$, higher global degree $d_G$, and higher pheromone $\tau$. An ant prefers to examine only the neighbors of the last vertex it visited. However, if every neighbor would violate the $k$ constraint, it defaults to sampling every $k$-feasible candidate.

The ACO algorithm is not guaranteed to find a $\theta$-feasible solution, even if one exists. This is a limitation of the $\theta$-heuristic as well (Section~3.2). However, like the $\theta$-heuristic, any returned solution is guaranteed $k$-feasible, and we empirically observe that ACO is more likely to return $\theta$-feasibly solutions.

\subsubsection{Algorithm Pseudocode}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:aco} is the colony loop. Here, $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+e^{-x})$ denotes the standard logistic sigmoid, used to dampen the impact of a vertex's degree with the entire graph as the size of the subgraph grows. We introduce a desirability function that prefers vertices with higher $d_S$, higher $d_G$, and higher pheromone $\tau$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Bipartite ACO for $k$-MDB}
\label{alg:aco}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
    \Require $G$; $A$, $T$, $\rho$, $\Delta\tau$
    \Ensure $B$, a $k$-DB of $G$
    \Function{$\eta$}{$u, S_i$}
        \State \Return $\tau(u) \times (d_S(u) + d_G(u) \times (1 - \sigma(|U_S| + |V_S|)))^3$
    \EndFunction
    \State $\tau \gets \mathbf{1}$; $B \gets \emptyset$
    \For{$e = 1,\dots,T$}
        \For{$i = 1,\dots,A$ \textbf{in parallel}}
        \State $S_i \gets \emptyset$; $C \gets V(G)$
        \While{$C \neq \emptyset$}
            \State $P \gets C \cap N(v_{\mathrm{last}})$ \textbf{if} nonempty \textbf{else} $C$
            \State sample $u \in P$ with $\Pr[u] \propto \eta(u, S_i)$
            \State $S_i \gets S_i \cup \{u\}$
            \State $C \gets \{w \in C \setminus \{u\} : |\bar E(S_i \cup \{w\})| \le k\}$
        \EndWhile
        \EndFor
        \State $S^* \gets \arg\max_{S \in \{S_1,\dots,S_A\}} |E(S)|$
        \State $B \gets \arg\max_{S \in \{B, S^*\}} |E(S)|$
        \State $\tau(u) \gets \tau(u) + \Delta\tau$ \textbf{for all} $u \in S^*$
        \State $\tau \gets (1-\rho)\,\tau$
    \EndFor
    \State \Return $B$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Time Complexity}

Let $n = |U| + |V|$ and $m = |E|$ for a bipartite graph. Each ant must first examine $n$ vertices. At every subsequent step, it will need to consider all vertices it can add to its subgraph without violating the $k$ constraint. A naive approach would examine $n - 1$ vertices, and then $n - 2$ vertices, etc. Thus, an ant will examine at most $n + (n - 1) + (n - 2) \ldots = n(n + 1)/2$. The upper time complexity would therefore be $\mathcal{O}(A \times T \times n^2)$ for $A$ ants advancing over $T$ epochs. In a parallelized implementation with $p$ processors, the true upper complexity bound is $\mathcal{O}(\lceil A / p \rceil \times T \times n^2)$. \section{Discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}

While ACO is substantially slower than the $\theta$-heuristic, it often finds solutions several times larger in edge count. Even small improvements ACO makes over the $\theta$-heuristic can meaningfully reduce the time that branch-and-pivot takes. Additionally, ACO had a higher $\theta$-feasibility rate than the $\theta$-heuristic, meaning it can be used as a valid lower bound more often. In cases where runtime is more critical, ant count $A$, epoch budget $T$, and replicate count $R$ can be reduced for a linear effect on wall-clock time, at the expense of solution size and $\theta$-feasibility (Section~4.1). 

ACO improves quality in several types of solutions. On several graphs, the $\theta$-heuristic returns a biclique with exactly $\theta$ vertices on one side, the structural family it was designed for. In many of these instances, ACO finds a substantially larger opposite side while still keeping one side at exactly $\theta$. In several cases, the ACO heuristic identified $\theta \times \theta$ $k$-DB on graphs that the $\theta$-heuristic failed to make $\theta$-feasible. Other wins are entirely different. ACO may escape the $\theta$-exact limitation.

We hypothesize this is due to ACO's exploration. Because the $\theta$-heuristic arbitrarily chooses $U$ as its $\theta$-side and greedily expands, early vertex choices can permanently shrink the retained neighborhood. Conversely, ACO's stochastic, massively parallel construction samples alternative $\theta \times \theta$ subgraphs and often recovers a better one. Even on the graphs it was designed for, the $\theta$-heuristic's greedy construction can be dominated by ACO search, which iteratively refines the $\theta$ vertices it uses.

\subsection{Limitations}

As described in Section~3.3, because branch-and-pivot runtime scales exponentially with vertex count, our seeding experiment cannot yet confirm whether the pruning benefit observed on small graphs extends to larger instances. Additionally, we could not validate ACO's solution quality against the absolute optimum on large graphs. Finally, we injected bicliques with $\theta$ vertices on both sides into the graphs. The $\theta$-heuristic may have performed better with a more realistic biclique of $\gg \theta$ vertices on one side. However, our observations that ACO can beat the $\theta$-heuristic on subgraphs with $\gg \theta$ vertices on only one side demonstrate that ACO is inherently a more explorative heuristic.

\section{Conclusion}

Ant Colony Optimization is a promising heuristic approach to identifying large $k$-DB. As an emerging field, there do not exist many tested heuristics for this problem. The $\theta$-heuristic, the only previously proposed heuristic in the literature, is significantly faster than our algorithm but often does not produce solutions as large. ACO offers an easily tunable quality--runtime tradeoff. One instance in which extra quality is worth extra time is in branch-and-pivot, where ACO can function as a much more effective lower bound, cutting total wall-clock-time by up to $56\%$ on the tested instances.

\subsection{Future Work}

A natural next step would be to rerun the seeded branch-and-pivot comparison of Section~4.2 on higher-performance hardware, so that the larger graphs excluded under the timeout in Section~3.3 can be included and the scaling of the seeding benefit can be tested directly.

Additionally, ACO could be adapted to have N subspecies that find N solutions in parallel by using a repulsion mechanism similar to that proposed for diversity in other ant colony variants [9], extending previous multi-colony parallelization strategies [10]. This could be an additional strength over the $\theta$-heuristic, taking advantage of its stochasticity to identify multiple solutions.
