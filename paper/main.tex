\begin{abstract}
The maximum $k$-defective edge biclique ($k$-MDB) problem is an emerging NP-hard optimization. We test three new heuristics which have different cost-quality tradeoffs. In many instances, these heuristics produce bicliques with up to $80\%$ more edges than previously proposed techniques. Finally, we identify novel graph reductions that significantly reduce graph size.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Bipartite graphs can model user-product behavior, person-place interaction, and more. The maximum edge biclique problem aims to identify the biclique with the most edges on a bipartite graph. The problem has applications in online recommendation systems, fraud detection, and community detection~\cite{cui}. However, real-world data often introduces noise to graphs, and structures with relatively few missing connections may still be relevant. A biclique that is allowed up to $k$ missing edges, or a $k$-defective biclique ($k$-DB), can overcome this real-world noise.

Similar dense subgraph models provide precedent for the $k$-MDB. In unipartite graphs, \emph{defective cliques}, cliques that are missing up to a certain number of edges, have been used to recover nearly complete structures from noisy biological interaction graphs~\cite{yu2006}. On bipartite graphs, relaxed models include $k$-biplexes, which permit each vertex up to $k$ nonneighbors in the biplex~\cite{yu2023}, and quasi-bicliques, which allows a maximum of $\gamma\%$ edges to be missing~\cite{TODO_quasi_biclique}. Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025} studied the problem of finding a $k$-defective biclique with the largest vertex count rather than edge count, which is also an NP-hard problem. The problem we study is similar to theirs, but it maximizes the number of edges. The $k$-defective maximum edge biclique generalizes to the maximum edge biclique when $k = 0$~\cite{cui,wang2025}.

Maximizing edge count alone is not enough for real-world interpretability. Without a lower bound on each side's size, the optimum is often a lopsided subgraph with many edges but little practical significance. In the case of fraud, a single popular item purchased by thousands of unrelated users could be a massive biclique with one vertex opposite many others, but it signals mass-market demand rather than a coordinated ring. We therefore seek only bicliques where both sides have at least $\theta$ vertices.

Ant Colony Optimization is a natural metaheuristic to apply to the $k$-defective, originally proposed by Dorigo et al.~\cite{dorigo1996}. We introduce an ACO heuristic for the maximum edge $k$-defective biclique problem, as well as two deterministic heuristics that beat the $\theta$-heuristic on several types of graphs.

The maximum edge $k$-defective biclique is an emerging NP-hard problem. To our knowledge, the $\theta$-heuristic of Cui et al.~\cite{cui} is the only published heuristic algorithm for this problem. We therefore benchmark three new heuristics against the only baseline in the literature. Additionally, to speed up heuristics and brute-force searches, we propose novel reduction methods. By mapping a single set of disjoint vertices onto a unipartite graph, we can significantly reduce graph size.

\section{Definitions}

Define a bipartite graph as a graph $G = (U, V, E)$, where $U$ and $V$ are two disjoint sets of vertices such that $|U| \leq |V|$, and $E \subseteq U \times V$ is the set of edges between $U$ and $V$. We use $u$ to refer to a vertex that lies on the $U$ side, $v$ for a vertex on the $V$ side, $z$ to refer to a vertex on either side, and $n$ to refer to $|U| + |V|$. Let $S = (U_S, V_S)$ denote a set of vertices where $U_S \subseteq U$ and $V_S \subseteq V$. Let $E_S = \{(u, v) \mid u \in U_S \wedge v \in V_S \wedge (u, v) \in E \}$ be the set of edges in a subgraph $S$. Define $\bar{E}_S = \{(u, v) \mid u \in U_S \wedge v \in V_S \wedge (u, v) \not\in E \}$ be the set of non-edges in a subgraph. Let a $k$-defective biclique be a subgraph $S$ where $|\bar{E}_S| \leq k$.

Define the neighbors of a vertex $z$ in a subgraph $S$ as $N_S(z) = \{w \in S \mid \{w, z\} \in E_S\}$. Define the nonneighbors of a vertex as $\bar{N}_S(z) = \{w \in S \mid (w, z) \not\in E_S\}$. Define the degree of a vertex as $d_S(z) = |N_S(z)|$, and the nondegree as $\bar{d}_S(z) = |\bar{N}_S(z)|$.

Define a $k$-defective biclique ($k$-DB) as a subgraph $S$ such that $\bar{E}_S \leq k$. Define a $\theta$-feasible $k$-DB as a subgraph $S = (U_S, V_S)$ such that $|U_S| \geq \theta$ and $|V_S| \geq \theta$ for a user-defined minimum size side $\theta$. Define the maximum $k$-MDB as the $\theta$-feasible $k$-DB with the largest number of edges in a graph $G$. We require that $\theta > k$ so that vertices must be connected to at least one vertex in the subgraph to be included in a $k$-MDB. The maximum clique reduces to the $k$-MDB, making the optimization variant of the latter NP-hard~\cite{cui}.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Baseline Heuristic}

As a baseline against our proposed algorithms, we implement the approach proposed by Cui et al.~\cite{cui}. These authors observe that many $\theta$-feasible $k$-DB have one side with exactly $\theta$ vertices. They therefore propose a heuristic that expands one side of the graph to $\theta$ vertices while peeling vertices on the other side to respect the $k$ constraint. The $\theta$-heuristic has a time complexity of $\mathcal{O}(\theta n + |E|)$~\cite{cui}.

\subsection{Graph Reduction Techniques}

Cui et al.~\cite{cui} propose common-neighbor reduction (CNR), which discards same-side vertex pairs with fewer than $\theta - k$ common neighbors, as well as any remaining vertex with degree less than $\theta - k$. We observe that CNR shrinks real-world graphs significantly, and use it as the base for the two reductions below. Both are slower than CNR, but they prune far more of the graph.

\subsubsection{One-Sided Representation}

A maximal $k$-DB is determined by the vertices on one of its sides. Fix $U_S \subseteq U$ with $|U_S| > k$. For $v \in V$, let its cost be the number of non-edges between $v$ and $U_S$:
\[
c(v \mid U_S) = |U_S| - |N(v) \cap U_S|.
\]
Because the non-edges of $v \in V$ are disjoint, the cost of a subset $V_S \subseteq V$ is $\sum_{v \in V_S}c(v \mid U_S)$. The best choice of $V_S$ is then to sort $V$ by cost and add vertices in ascending order until the total cost exceeds $k$. Both the reductions and ACO can therefore search over subsets of one side only. The edge count of the full biclique follows from the greedy fill.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{GreedyFill}
\label{alg:greedy-fill}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
   \Require $G = (U, V, E)$; $U_S \subseteq U$; $k$
   \Ensure $(U_S, V_S)$ with the most edges among all $k$-DB with left side $U_S$

   \State $c(v) \gets |U_S| - |N(v) \cap U_S|$ \textbf{for each} $v \in V$
   \State sort $V$ by $c$ ascending; \quad $V_S \gets \emptyset$; \quad $b \gets k$
   \For{$v \in V$ in sorted order}
       \If{$c(v) > b$ \textbf{or} $c(v) = |U_S|$} \textbf{break} \EndIf
       \State $V_S \gets V_S \cup \{v\}$; \quad $b \gets b - c(v)$
   \EndFor
   \State \Return $(U_S, V_S)$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The naive time complexity of \ref{alg:greedy-fill} is $\mathcal{O}(|V| + |E|)$. However, a memoized approach can reduce the time complexity of greedy fill as a subgraph is incrementally added. By keeping track of the degree of every candidate vertex in a subgraph $U_S \subseteq U$, the cost of computing $N_S(v)$ for a given vertex $v$ goes from $\mathcal{O}(d_S(v))$ to $\mathcal{O}(1)$, and therefore the memoized complexity of \ref{alg:greedy-fill} is $\mathcal{O}(d(v) + |V|)$ when adding a vertex $v$.

\subsubsection{\texorpdfstring{$\theta$}{θ} Neighbor Reduction}

Let $C(u, u') = N_H(u) \cap N_H(u')$ be the common neighbors of two same-side vertices. Define the mapped graph $H = (U, \tilde{E})$, where $uu' \in \tilde{E}$ if $|C(u, u')| \ge \theta - k$.

If $u$ and $u'$ both appear in a $\theta$-feasible $k$-DB $D$, then each of the at least $\theta$ vertices in $V_D$ is adjacent to both, except for those incident to a missing edge. At most $k$ vertices can be, so $|C(u, u')| \ge \theta - k$. Therefore $U_D$ is a clique of at least $\theta$ vertices in $H$, which gives two pruning rules:
\begin{itemize}
   \item A vertex with degree less than $\theta - 1$ in $H$ cannot be in a clique of size $\theta$, and can be removed.
   \item An edge $uu'$ with fewer than $\theta - 2$ common neighbors in $H$ ($|N(u) \cap N(u')| < \theta - 2$) cannot be in a clique of size $\theta$, and can be removed.
\end{itemize}
Each removal can lower the degrees of other vertices, so both rules are applied until no vertex or edge changes.

\subsubsection{Missing Edge Reduction}
\label{sec:missing-edge-reduction}

On the remaining edges of $H$, assign a weight
\[
w(u, u') = \max\bigl(0,\ \theta - |C(u, u')|\bigr).
\]
$|C(u, u')|$ is the largest number of edges that $u$ and $u'$ could contribute to a $k$-DB. $|V_D| \geq \theta$, so a lower bound on the number of missing edges that $u$ and $u'$ contribute to $D$ is $\theta - |C(u, u')|$. Because this is a lower bound, we floor this estimate at 0.

Take three vertices $u, u', u'' \in U_D$. $w(u, u')$ is the number of missing edges that $u$ and $u'$ would contribute, but $w(u, u') + w(u, u'') + w(u', u'')$ would overcount. For instance, if on a bipartite graph with three vertices each there is only one missing edge, the missing edge would be counted twice: once for $uu'$ and once for $uz$. For a given $u$, its missing edges may be overcounted on any other edge it's connected to. Because in the graph $\{u, u', z\}$, each vertex has exactly two edges, each edge may be overcounted by a maximum factor of $1/2$. Hence
\[
|\bar{E}(D)| \ge \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(w(u, u') + w(u, z) + w(u', z)\bigr)
\]
Since $|U_D| \ge \theta \ge 3$, every $k$-DB containing $u$ and $u'$ contains some $z \in C_H(u, u')$, where $C_H$ is the common neighborhood in $H$. We therefore prune the edge $uu'$ if
\[
\min_{z \in C_H(u, u')} \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(w(u, u') + w(u, z) + w(u', z)\bigr) > k.
\]
As with $\theta$ neighbor reduction, this is repeated until the graph stops changing, since removing edges changes common neighborhoods and weights.

\subsection{Double-Greedy Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Double-Greedy Algorithm for $k$-MDB}
\label{alg:double-greedy}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
   \Require $G = (U, V, E)$; $k$, $\theta$
   \Ensure $S = (U_S, V_S)$, a $k$-DB of $G$

   \State $z \gets \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{x \in U \cup V} d_G(x)$
   \State $S \gets (\{z\}, N(z))$ \textbf{if} $z \in U$ \textbf{else} $(N(z), \{z\})$

   \While{true}
       \If{$\min(|U_S|, |V_S|) < \theta$}
           \State $P \gets U \setminus U_S$ \textbf{if} $|U_S| \le |V_S|$ \textbf{else} $V \setminus V_S$
           \Comment{only grow the smaller side}
       \Else
           \State $P \gets (U \cup V) \setminus S$
       \EndIf

       \State $C \gets \{z \in P : |\bar{E}(S \cup \{z\})| \le k\}$
       \Comment{$k$-feasible candidates}
       \If{$C = \emptyset$}
           \State \textbf{break}
       \EndIf

       \State $z^* \gets \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{z \in C} |E(S \cup \{z\})|$
       \State $S \gets S \cup \{z^*\}$
       \Comment{$z^*$ joins $U_S$ if $z^* \in U$, else $V_S$}
   \EndWhile

   \State \Return $S$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Time Complexity}
The Double-Greedy algorithm scores every candidate vertex, which costs $\mathcal{O}(|E|)$. It repeats this process until it builds a large solution. Every step loops through the graph to generate a list of candidates,  and therefore a naive implementation has a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(n \times |E|)$. A memoized implementation can update the subgraph count by tracking $d_S$ for every vertex. Each time a vertex is added to the solution, its neighbors add $1$ to their $d_S$. The number of edges visited is equal to the total degree of each of the vertices in $S$, which is $\mathcal{O}(|E|)$. Each step loops over every $z \in G$ to generate a candidate step. The number of steps is equal to the size of the solution, which is $n$ in the worst case. Therefore, a memoized implementation has a complexity of $\mathcal{O}(n^2 + |E|)$. Finally, the maximum size of any one side of a $k$-DB is $\min(|V|, \Delta + k)$, where $\Delta$ is the maximum degree of a vertex. Therefore, naive implementation has a time complexity of $\mathcal{O}(\min(|V|, \Delta + k) \times |E|)$. Therefore, an optimized complexity is $\mathcal{O}(\min(|V|, \Delta + k) \times n + |E|)$, with space $\mathcal{O}(n)$.

\subsection{Clique-Edge Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Clique-Edge}
\label{alg:clique-edge}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
   \Require $G = (U, V, E)$ with $|U| \le |V|$; $k$, $\theta$
   \Ensure $D = (U_D, V_D)$, a $k$-DB of $G$

   \State $H \gets (U, \tilde{E})$ with $uu' \in \tilde{E}$ iff $|C(u,u')| \ge \theta - k$
   \Comment{weight $\omega(uu') = |C(u,u')|$}
   \State $uu' \gets \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{e \in \tilde{E}} \omega(e)$
   \State $S \gets \{u, u'\}$; \quad $x \gets \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{y \in S} d_H(y)$
   \State $D \gets \emptyset$
   \While{true}
       \State $R \gets N_H(x) \setminus S$
       \If{$R = \emptyset$}
           \State $R \gets N_H(S) \setminus S$
           \Comment{fall back to the whole frontier}
       \EndIf
       \If{$R = \emptyset$} \textbf{break} \EndIf

       \State $(s, p) \gets \operatorname*{arg\,max}_{s \in S,\, p \in R,\, sp \in \tilde{E}} \omega(sp)$
       \State $T \gets \textsc{GreedyFill}(S \cup \{p\})$
       \If{$|V_T| \ge \theta$}
           \State $S \gets S \cup \{p\}$; \quad $x \gets p$
           \If{$|S| \geq \theta$ \textbf{and} $|E(T)| > |E(D)|$}
               \State$D \gets T$
           \EndIf
       \Else
           \State remove $p$ from $H$
           \Comment{infeasibility is monotone in $S$}
       \EndIf
   \EndWhile

   \State \Return $D$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Time Complexity} Let $n = |U| + |V|$, $m = |E|$, and let $h = |\tilde{E}|$ be the number of edges in the mapped graph $H$. Building $H$ requires $|C(u,u')|$ for every pair $u,u' \in U$. Each $v \in V$ is a common neighbor of every pair in $N(v)$, so enumerating these pairs for all $v \in V$ takes $O(\sum_{v \in V} d(v)^2)$. Each iteration of the main loop either adds a vertex $p$ to $S$ or removes $p$ from $H$. A vertex in $S$ is never removed, and a removed vertex never joins $S$, so each vertex of $U$ is considered as $p$ at most once and the loop runs at most $|U|$ times. In each iteration, lines 6–10 scan the edges of $H$ incident to $S$, which costs $O(h)$. GreedyFill (Algorithm 1) can be evaluated in $O(d(p) + |V|)$ by maintaining $n(v) = |N(v) \cap U_S|$ for each $v \in V$. Adding $p$ to $U_S$ increments $n(v)$ for each $v \in N(p)$, after which $c(v) = |U_S| - n(v)$ is available in $O(1)$. Since $c(v) > k$ excludes $v$ from $V_T$, a single pass over $V$ places each remaining vertex into one of $k+1$ buckets by cost. Draining the buckets in ascending order until the budget is exhausted then yields $V_T$ and $|E(T)|$ in $O(|V| + k) = O(|V|)$. If $p$ is rejected, the increments are undone at the same $O(d(p))$ cost. Because each vertex is considered as $p$ at most once, the updates to $n(v)$ total $O(m)$ over the whole run. The total time complexity is therefore

$$
O\left(\sum_{v \in V} d(v)^2 + |U|(h + |V|) + m\right),
$$

with space $O(h + m)$. In the worst case, $h \leq |U|^2$ and $\sum_{v \in V} d(v)^2 \leq |V| \cdot |U|^2$, and since $|U| \leq |V|$, this gives $O(|U|^2|V|) \subseteq O(n^3)$. On real graphs, CNR removes most vertices and edges before Clique-Edge runs (Table III), so $|U|$, $|V|$, and $h$ are much smaller than these bounds suggest.

\subsection{ACO Algorithm}

\subsubsection{Overview of Algorithm}

ACO builds a $k$-DB with a colony of $A$ ants. Each ant grows a subgraph $S$ one vertex at a time, sampling the next vertex with probability proportional to a desirability $\eta$ that increases with subgraph degree $d_S$, global degree $d_G$, and pheromone $\tau$. An ant first considers only the $k$-feasible neighbors of its last vertex, falling back to all $k$-feasible candidates.

\subsubsection{Algorithm Pseudocode}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:aco} is the colony loop. Here, $\sigma(x) = 1/(1+e^{-x})$ denotes the standard logistic sigmoid.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{ACO for $k$-MDB}
\label{alg:aco}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
   \Require $G$; $A$, $T$, $\rho$, $\Delta\tau$
   \Ensure $B$, a $k$-DB of $G$
   \Function{$\eta$}{$z, S_i$}
       \State \Return $\tau(z) \times (d_S(z) + d_G(z) \times (1 - \sigma(|U_S| + |V_S|)))^3$
   \EndFunction
   \State $\tau \gets \mathbf{1}$; $B \gets \emptyset$
   \For{$e = 1,\dots,T$}
       \For{$i = 1,\dots,A$ \textbf{in parallel}}
       \State $S_i \gets \emptyset$; $C \gets V(G)$
       \While{$C \neq \emptyset$}
           \State $P \gets C \cap N(z_{\mathrm{last}})$ \textbf{if} nonempty \textbf{else} $C$
           \State sample $z \in P$ with $\Pr[z] \propto \eta(z, S_i)$
           \State $S_i \gets S_i \cup \{z\}$
           \State $C \gets \{w \in C \setminus \{z\} : |\bar E(S_i \cup \{w\})| \le k\}$
       \EndWhile
       \EndFor
       \State $S^* \gets \arg\max_{S \in \{S_1,\dots,S_A\}} |E(S)|$
       \State $B \gets \arg\max_{S \in \{B, S^*\}} |E(S)|$
       \State $\tau(z) \gets \tau(z) + \Delta\tau$ \textbf{for all} $z \in S^*$
       \State $\tau \gets (1-\rho)\,\tau$
   \EndFor
   \State \Return $B$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Theoretical Time Complexity}

Let $n = |U| + |V|$ and $m = |E|$ for a bipartite graph. Each ant must first examine $n$ vertices. A single vertex's neighbors may not have any $k$-feasible additions, so in the worst case, an ant must consider all vertices it can add to its subgraph at every step. As described in Section~3.3, the maximum number of vertices in a $k$-MDB is $\mathcal{O}(\min(n, \Delta + k))$. An ant therefore examines $\mathcal{O}(\min(n, \Delta + k) \times n)$ vertices. Additionally, it prefers to examine the [TOMORROW, BE SURE TO TALK ABOUT THE $k \times |V|$ FOR THE GREEDY ALGORITHM, REMOVE THE GREEDY FILL INFORMATION CURRENTLY IN CLIQUE-EDGE AND PUT IT RIGHT NEXT TO GREEDY FILL / TALK ABOUT IT IN THE GREEDY FILL SECTION, FINISH THIS SENTENCE ABOUT HOW ANTS PREFER THE NEIGHBORS SO IT'S + $m$, ALSO PUT THE BIT ABOUT MEMOIZING SUBGRAPH DEGREE IN AN ABOVE BULLET POINT SO I DON'T HAVE TO REPEAT IT AND EVERY ALGORITHM CAN USE THAT IDEA]. Ants also run independently, only syncing pheromone at the end of each epoch, so the upper time complexity is therefore $\mathcal{O}(\lceil A / p \rceil \times T \times (\min(n, \Delta + k) \times n + m))$ on a computer with $p$ processors. In practice, $\Delta + k \ll n$, so this does not scale quadratically with $n$.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}


Unless otherwise noted, all ACO experiments use $A = 100, \rho = 0.05, \Delta\tau = 0.05, T = 5, R = 5$. ACO is stochastic, so we report the best solution over $R$ independent ``replicates'', each with its own ant population and pheromone state, and its runtime includes all five. One warmup replicate per trial is discarded to exclude Julia JIT compilation.


We test on 37 graphs from two public collections: 28 from \href{http://konect.cc}{KONECT}, a standard repository of real-world bipartite networks; and 9 from \href{http://amazon-reviews-2023.github.io}{Amazon Reviews 2023}, user-product review graphs in selected product categories. All tests were run on a computer with Ubuntu, an Intel(R) Core(TM) Ultra 7 258V CPU, 8 threads, and 32 gigabytes of RAM.

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}

While ACO is substantially slower than the $\theta$-heuristic, it often finds solutions several times larger in edge count. Even small improvements ACO makes over the $\theta$-heuristic can meaningfully reduce the time that branch-and-pivot takes. Additionally, ACO had a higher $\theta$-feasibility rate than the $\theta$-heuristic, meaning it can be used as a valid lower bound more often. In cases where runtime is more critical, ant count $A$, epoch budget $T$, and replicate count $R$ can be reduced for a linear effect on wall-clock time, at the expense of solution size and $\theta$-feasibility (Section~4.1).

ACO improves quality in several types of solutions. On several graphs, both ACO and the $\theta$-heuristic find a solution with exactly $\theta$ vertices on one side, but ACO finds a substantially larger opposite side. In other cases, ACO identified $\theta \times \theta$ $k$-DB on graphs that the $\theta$-heuristic failed to make $\theta$-feasible. ACO may escape the $\theta$-exact limitation entirely, finding $k$-DB with both sides larger than $\theta$ vertices that the $\theta$-heuristic could not have found.

We hypothesize this is due to ACO's exploration. Because the $\theta$-heuristic arbitrarily chooses $U$ as its $\theta$-side and greedily expands, early vertex choices can permanently shrink the retained neighborhood. Conversely, ACO's stochastic, massively parallel construction samples alternative $\theta \times \theta$ cores and often recovers a better one. Thus, ACO often beats the $\theta$-heuristic even on structures the latter was designed for.

\subsection{Limitations}

As described in Section~3.3, because branch-and-pivot runtime scales exponentially with vertex count, our seeding experiment cannot yet confirm whether the pruning benefit observed on small graphs extends to larger instances. Additionally, we could not validate ACO's solution quality against the absolute optimum on large graphs. Finally, we injected bicliques with $\theta$ vertices on both sides into the graphs. The $\theta$-heuristic may have performed better with a more realistic biclique of $\gg \theta$ vertices on one side. However, our observations that ACO can beat the $\theta$-heuristic on subgraphs with $\gg \theta$ vertices on only one side demonstrate that ACO is inherently a more explorative heuristic.

\section{Conclusion}

Ant Colony Optimization is a promising heuristic approach to identifying large $k$-DB. As an emerging field, there do not exist many tested heuristics for this problem. The $\theta$-heuristic, the only previously proposed heuristic in the literature, is significantly faster than our algorithm but often does not produce solutions as large. ACO offers an easily tunable quality--runtime tradeoff. One instance in which extra quality is worth extra time is in branch-and-pivot, where ACO can function as a much more effective lower bound, cutting total wall-clock-time by up to $56\%$ on the tested instances.

\subsection{Future Work}

A natural next step would be to rerun the seeded branch-and-pivot comparison of Section~4.2 on higher-performance hardware, so that the larger graphs excluded under the timeout in Section~3.3 can be included and the scaling of the seeding benefit can be tested directly. Additionally, ACO could be adapted to have N subspecies that find N solutions in parallel by using a repulsion mechanism similar to that proposed for diversity in other ant colony variants~\cite{tfaili2008}, extending previous multi-colony parallelization strategies~\cite{middendorf2002}.

Additionally, we observe that in \ref{sec:missing-edge-reduction}, if
\[
(|N_H| + |\{u, u'\}| + 1)^{-1}\sum_{A, B \in N_H}w(A, B) > k
\]
the edge on the mapped graph can be pruned. This could be a way to prune more of the graph, although at a slower cost.
